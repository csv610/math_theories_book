\paragraph{Alexander Sushkevich and David Rees}
Sushkevich studied the structure of finite semigroups, while Rees described important completely simple semigroups through matrix-like constructions. Their work showed how associative composition can be analyzed without assuming inverses.

\paragraph{James Alexander Green and automata theorists}
Green introduced the relations that organize semigroup elements by the principal ideals they generate. Later work connected these relations with finite automata, transformation systems, and operator theory, where irreversible composition is the central example \cite{semigroup_ref1}.

\section*{3. Theory}
\subsection*{Core theory}
\paragraph{Definition and examples}
A semigroup is a set $S$ with a product satisfying
\[
(ab)c=a(bc)
\]
for all $a,b,c\in S$. No identity and no inverse are required. String concatenation is associative: grouping the joins differently does not change the final string. Functions under composition form a semigroup, as do square matrices under multiplication and positive integers under addition.

A monoid is a semigroup with an identity. A group is a monoid in which every element has an inverse. Thus semigroups generalize groups by allowing irreversible processes such as a reset button, a lossy computation, or a function that is not reversible \cite{semigroup_ref1}.

\paragraph{History and structure}
The systematic study began in the early twentieth century. Anton Sushkevich studied finite simple semigroups; David Rees and James Green developed structural tools; Alfred Clifford and Gordon Preston published foundational monographs. Green's relations divide a semigroup according to the principal ideals generated by its elements \cite{semigroup_ref2}.

An ideal is a subset absorbing multiplication from the relevant side. Minimal ideals describe the long-term core of a finite semigroup. In a finite semigroup, repeated products eventually repeat, so powers of an element enter a periodic part. This finite behavior is useful when a process is applied repeatedly.

\paragraph{Representations and finite semigroups}
Cayley's theorem for semigroups represents every semigroup as a semigroup of transformations of a set. An element is therefore a function, and multiplication is composition. This generalizes the group representation by permutations, replacing reversible bijections with arbitrary maps.

Finite semigroups are closely related to finite automata. A word read by an automaton induces a transformation of its state set. Words with the same effect form a syntactic monoid, and algebraic properties of this monoid characterize language classes. Krohn--Rhodes theory decomposes finite semigroups into simpler group and aperiodic components, analogous in spirit to factoring \cite{semigroup_ref3}.

\paragraph{Automata and formal languages}
The connection between semigroups and automata theory is precise and deep. A deterministic finite automaton (DFA) reads input symbols from a finite alphabet $\Sigma$ and updates its state according to a transition function $\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to Q$. Each symbol $a\in\Sigma$ therefore defines a transformation $q\mapsto\delta(q,a)$ of the state set $Q$. Concatenating symbols corresponds to composing these transformations, so every word $w\in\Sigma^*$ acts on $Q$ as a transformation. The set of all transformations obtained from words forms the transition semigroup of the automaton \cite{semigroup_ref3}.

The syntactic monoid of a regular language $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$ is the quotient of the free monoid $\Sigma^*$ by the two-sided congruence that identifies words having the same effect in every left and right context of $L$. A fundamental theorem states that a language is regular if and only if its syntactic monoid is finite. More refined algebraic conditions on the syntactic monoid characterize proper sub-classes: a language is star-free if and only if its syntactic monoid is aperiodic (has no nontrivial group subgroups), and a language is piecewise testable if and only if its syntactic monoid is $\mathcal J$-trivial. Krohn--Rhodes theory then decomposes the transition semigroup of any DFA into group and aperiodic components, with each component recognizing a specific aspect of the language's structure. This algebraic decomposition provides both a classification tool and a design principle for automata \cite{semigroup_ref2,semigroup_ref3}.

\paragraph{Analysis and differential equations}
A one-parameter operator semigroup is a family $T(t)$ with $T(0)=I$ and $T(t+s)=T(t)T(s)$ for $t,s\geq0$. It describes evolution forward in time. Unlike a group, negative time may not be possible. If $A$ is the generator, formally $T(t)=e^{tA}$.

The heat equation is an example. Starting with a temperature distribution $u(0)$, the heat semigroup sends it to $u(t)$ for later times. Diffusion smooths irregularities and loses information, so reversing it is unstable. Semigroup methods make existence, uniqueness, and stability of evolution equations precise \cite{semigroup_ref4}.

\section*{4. Important theorems and results}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Cayley representation theorem.} Every semigroup is isomorphic to a semigroup of transformations under composition.

\item \textbf{Green's relations.} The relations $\mathcal L,\mathcal R,\mathcal H,\mathcal D,\mathcal J$ organize elements by their principal left, right, and two-sided ideals.

\item \textbf{Krohn--Rhodes theorem.} Finite semigroups admit decompositions into group components and aperiodic components in a precise wreath-product sense.

\item \textbf{Syntactic monoid theorem.} A regular language has a finite syntactic monoid, and its algebra records the language's behavior under contexts.

\item \textbf{Hille--Yosida principle.} Suitable conditions on an operator characterize when it generates a strongly continuous semigroup.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent\emph{Source for these results:} \cite{semigroup_ref1}.

\paragraph{Application: image processing through mathematical morphology}
Mathematical morphology, a framework for image analysis, provides a direct application of semigroup theory. In this setting an image is a function from a discrete grid to a set of intensity values, and basic operations are defined by a structuring element $B$. Dilation of an image $f$ by $B$ is the operation
\[
(f\oplus B)(x)=\max_{y\in B}f(x-y),
\]
and erosion is
\[
(f\ominus B)(x)=\min_{y\in B}f(x+y).
\]
When the structuring element is fixed, successive dilations compose associatively:
\[
(f\oplus B)\oplus C=f\oplus(B\oplus C),
\]
so the set of all dilations by elements from a fixed collection forms a semigroup under composition. Because dilation is generally not invertible---information about the original image can be lost---the inverse operation does not exist, and the algebraic structure is genuinely a semigroup rather than a group. Composite operations such as opening (erosion followed by dilation) and closing (dilation followed by erosion) are elements of this semigroup. The algebraic axioms of associativity guarantee that multi-step morphological filtering pipelines can be analysed and simplified by rearranging the semigroup product, which is the basis for efficient hardware and software implementations in image processing \cite{semigroup_ref1,semigroup_ref2}.

\section*{Glossary}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline,itemsep=0.3em]
\item[\textbf{Semigroup.}] A set equipped with an associative binary operation but not required to have an identity element or inverses, modelling irreversible sequential processes.
\item[\textbf{Monoid.}] A semigroup that possesses an identity element; every group is a monoid, but a monoid need not have inverses.
\item[\textbf{Green's relations.}] Five equivalence relations $\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{J}$ that partition a semigroup according to the principal left, right, and two-sided ideals generated by its elements.
\item[\textbf{Syntactic monoid.}] The finite monoid obtained from a regular language by identifying words that have the same effect in every left and right context; its algebraic properties classify the language.
\item[\textbf{Operator semigroup.}] A one-parameter family $T(t)$ of linear operators satisfying $T(0)=I$ and $T(t+s)=T(t)T(s)$, describing forward-in-time evolution such as heat diffusion.
\item[\textbf{Krohn--Rhodes theorem.}] The decomposition of a finite semigroup into a wreath product of simple group and aperiodic components, analogous to factoring an integer into primes.
\item[\textbf{Aperiodic semigroup.}] A finite semigroup in which every element's powers eventually stabilize to a single idempotent, with no nontrivial group structure present.
\item[\textbf{Transition semigroup.}] The semigroup of transformations of an automaton's state set induced by reading all possible input words.
\item[\textbf{Idempotent.}] An element $e$ satisfying $e^2=e$; in finite semigroups, every element's powers eventually reach an idempotent.
\item[\textbf{Regular language.}] A formal language recognizable by a deterministic finite automaton; its syntactic monoid is finite.
\item[\textbf{Wreath product.}] An algebraic construction combining two semigroups formalizing the Krohn--Rhodes decomposition.
\item[\textbf{Generator.}] The operator $A$ from which a semigroup is built via $T(t)=e^{tA}$; its properties determine existence and uniqueness.
\end{description}
\section*{7. Sample Questions}
\emph{Exercise reference:} \cite{semigroup_ref1}. The cited edition is the source for the exercise set; consult its Exercises section for the official statement and numbering.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.9em]
\item \textbf{Exercise 1.} Let $S=\{a,b\}$ with the multiplication table $a\cdot a=a$, $a\cdot b=b\cdot a=b\cdot b=a$. Verify that $S$ is a semigroup (i.e., multiplication is associative) and identify the idempotent elements. \emph{Solution.} Check all triple products. $(a\cdot a)\cdot a=a=a\cdot(a\cdot a)$; $(a\cdot a)\cdot b=a\cdot b=b=a\cdot b=a\cdot(a\cdot b)$; $(a\cdot b)\cdot a=b\cdot a=b=a\cdot b=a\cdot(b\cdot a)$; $(a\cdot b)\cdot b=b\cdot b=a=a\cdot b=a\cdot(b\cdot b)$; $(b\cdot a)\cdot a=b\cdot a=b=b\cdot a=(b\cdot a)\cdot a$; $(b\cdot a)\cdot b=b\cdot b=a=b\cdot a=b\cdot(a\cdot b)$; $(b\cdot b)\cdot a=a\cdot a=a=b\cdot a=(b\cdot b)\cdot a$; $(b\cdot b)\cdot b=a\cdot b=b=b\cdot b=(b\cdot b)\cdot b$. All cases check: $S$ is a semigroup. An idempotent satisfies $x^2=x$: $a^2=a$ so $a$ is idempotent; $b^2=a\ne b$ so $b$ is not.

\item \textbf{Exercise 2.} Consider the transition semigroup of a DFA with states $\{q_0,q_1\}$, alphabet $\{a,b\}$, and transitions $\delta(q_0,a)=q_1$, $\delta(q_0,b)=q_0$, $\delta(q_1,a)=q_0$, $\delta(q_1,b)=q_1$. Write the transformation on $\{q_0,q_1\}$ induced by each of the four words $a,b,ab,ba$. \emph{Solution.} Word $a$: $q_0\mapsto q_1$, $q_1\mapsto q_0$, i.e., the transposition $(q_0\;q_1)$. Word $b$: $q_0\mapsto q_0$, $q_1\mapsto q_1$, i.e., the identity. Word $ab$: first apply $b$ (identity), then $a$ (transposition), giving $(q_0\;q_1)$. Word $ba$: first apply $a$ (transposition), then $b$ (identity), giving $(q_0\;q_1)$. The transition semigroup consists of $\{id,(q_0\;q_1)\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

\item \textbf{Exercise 3.} Let $S$ be the set of $2\times 2$ matrices $\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}$ under matrix multiplication. Verify closure and identify all idempotent elements. \emph{Solution.} $I\cdot A=A\cdot I=A$ for all $A$. $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, so this matrix is idempotent. $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, so the zero matrix is idempotent. $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$; $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Closure holds. All three elements are idempotent.

\item \textbf{Exercise 4.} Consider the right-zero semigroup $S=\{a,b,c\}$ with $x\cdot y=y$ for all $x,y\in S$. Compute the Green's $\mathcal{R}$-classes and $\mathcal{L}$-classes of $S$. \emph{Solution.} For $\mathcal{R}$: $x\,\mathcal{R}\,y$ iff $xS^1=yS^1$. Since $x\cdot y=y$ for all $x,y$, we have $xS^1=S$ for every $x$. So all elements are $\mathcal{R}$-equivalent: there is one $\mathcal{R}$-class, $S$ itself. For $\mathcal{L}$: $x\,\mathcal{L}\,y$ iff $S^1 x=S^1 y$. $S^1 x=\{x\}$ since $y\cdot x=x$ for all $y$. So each element forms its own $\mathcal{L}$-class: $\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}$.

\item \textbf{Exercise 5.} Let $T=\{0,1\}$ with multiplication given by $x\cdot y=\min(x,y)$ (the \emph{meet} semigroup). Find the subsemigroups of $T$. \emph{Solution.} The subsets closed under $\min$ are: $\varnothing$ (not a subsemigroup, as semigroups are nonempty by convention), $\{0\}$ (since $\min(0,0)=0$), $\{1\}$ (since $\min(1,1)=1$), and $\{0,1\}$ (the full semigroup). The subsemigroups are $\{0\}$, $\{1\}$, and $\{0,1\}$.

\end{enumerate}
